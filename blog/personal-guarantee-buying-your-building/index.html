The Personal Guarantee: What You're Really Signing When You Finance Your Building

Somewhere in the closing package for your building loan is a document that matters more than the note itself. It is usually a page or two, it is easy to skim past on the way to the signature line, and it is the reason buying your building is not actually a limited-liability decision. It is the personal guarantee, and almost no owner-occupied commercial loan closes without one.

Why the LLC Does Not Protect You Here

Most business owners buy their building through an LLC specifically for the liability shield it provides. That shield works fine for a slip-and-fall claim or a vendor dispute. It does almost nothing for the mortgage. Lenders know an LLC has no track record, no credit history, and often no assets beyond the building being financed. So before they lend six or seven figures against it, they ask the humans behind the entity to stand behind the debt personally.

That is the personal guarantee: a separate contract, signed alongside the loan, in which you agree that if the LLC cannot pay, you will — out of your personal assets, not just your investment in the business.

Who Actually Has to Sign

The rule is consistent across nearly every SBA-backed program used to buy owner-occupied real estate: anyone who owns 20% or more of the borrowing entity, directly or indirectly, must sign an unlimited personal guarantee. There is no negotiating this threshold and no cap the lender can offer instead — SBA SOP guidance sets it, and the lender has to comply.

Two details catch owners off guard. First, ownership is aggregated across spouses. If you hold 15% of the entity and your spouse holds 6%, your household crosses 20% and both of you guarantee the full balance, even though neither of you individually cleared the line. Second, "unlimited" means exactly that. There is no dollar ceiling written into the guarantee. If the loan balance is $1.4 million at the time of default, plus accrued interest, late fees, and the lender's collection costs, that is the number you are on the hook for — not the equity you put in, not a percentage of the loan, all of it.

Conventional bank loans work the same way in practice, even without an SBA program forcing the issue. A community bank underwriting a $2 million owner-occupied purchase will almost always require a full guarantee from every principal owner, because the alternative — lending against a single-asset LLC with no other recourse — is a risk profile banks price very poorly or decline outright.

What "Full Recourse" Means in a Bad Year

Run the scenario forward. Your business hits a rough stretch, revenue drops, and the LLC misses payments on the building loan. The lender does not have to exhaust the building first. Depending on the guarantee language, it can pursue your personal bank accounts, your investment portfolio, and other real estate you own, in parallel with foreclosing on the building — not sequentially after it. A deficiency judgment, if the eventual sale of the building does not cover the full balance, follows you personally even after the property is gone.

This is the detail that separates buying a building from renting one, and it is worth sitting with before you sign, not after. Renting caps your downside at the lease term and the security deposit. Owning with a personal guarantee removes that cap entirely. The upside — equity, appreciation, the tax treatment covered in the tax advantages of ownership — is real, but it is not free of this risk, and no amount of financing structure eliminates it. It only changes who else is exposed alongside you.

The Places You Can Actually Negotiate

You cannot negotiate away the guarantee itself on an SBA-backed deal, and you will rarely succeed on a conventional bank loan either. But a few terms around the edges are genuinely up for discussion, and most owners never ask.

Spousal carve-outs. If your spouse has no operational role and no ownership stake at all, some lenders will accept a guarantee from you alone rather than aggregating household ownership — but only if the spouse's ownership is actually zero, not simply small. Structure ownership percentages before closing, not after.

Guarantee release triggers. On a conventional loan, ask whether the guarantee can be released or reduced once the loan-to-value ratio drops below a set threshold, typically 65-70%, after several years of paydown and appreciation. Banks will not offer this unprompted, but many will write it in if you ask before signing.

Carve-outs for specific liabilities. Environmental contamination, fraud, and misapplied insurance proceeds are the standard "bad boy" carve-outs lenders add on top of the base guarantee. Read them closely — a poorly drafted carve-out can turn what looks like a limited guarantee into an unlimited one under certain conditions.

Which entity signs, not whether one does. If you already run the OpCo/PropCo split, structure it so the guarantee sits with the operating entity's principals rather than pulling in a holding company that owns unrelated assets you would rather keep insulated.

Insuring Around the Exposure

You cannot insure away a personal guarantee directly, but you can reduce the odds you ever need to test it. Adequate business interruption coverage, a cash reserve sized to 6-12 months of debt service sitting outside the operating account, and keeping personal and business liabilities cleanly separated all matter more once you understand what a default actually exposes. Some owners also carry key-person life insurance sized to retire the guaranteed balance, so a death does not leave a surviving spouse holding a mortgage guarantee on top of everything else.

Sign With Your Eyes Open

None of this is a reason to avoid buying your building. Every serious real estate purchase a business owner makes involves a personal guarantee in some form, and the wealth-building case for ownership covered throughout this site holds up under that reality. The point is not to be afraid of the guarantee. It is to read it, know exactly what it exposes, negotiate the few terms that are actually negotiable, and go into the closing as informed as the lender's attorney who drafted it. Knowing what lenders evaluate before you apply puts you in a stronger position to ask for the carve-outs that matter.
