Commercial Rent vs. Ownership: Build an Occupancy Cost Bridge

Compare commercial lease costs with ownership cash requirements, including maintenance, capital work, transaction costs, and business flexibility.

Comparing monthly rent with a monthly mortgage payment leaves out much of the decision to buy a business property. Rent may include services the owner would have to procure separately. Ownership can require cash for repairs and improvements long before those costs appear in an annual budget.

Build an occupancy cost bridge for the same usable space, time horizon, and operating needs. The purpose is to explain every difference between the two alternatives. A property that looks cheaper on monthly payments may consume cash the operating business needs for hiring, equipment, or inventory.

Normalize the lease alternative

Start with the actual lease. List base rent, escalations, common-area charges, taxes, insurance responsibilities, maintenance obligations, and utilities. Mark amounts that can change at reconciliation. Add tenant improvements and moving expenses that would be required under the alternative being evaluated.

Include the lease's practical limits: renewal timing, expansion rights, permitted use, and responsibility for major equipment. Ask the real-estate team to interpret obligations rather than assuming all leases allocate costs in the same way.

Build the ownership alternative separately

List financing payments, property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance contracts, management time, and a schedule of likely capital projects. Show purchase cash, inspections, legal and closing costs, and initial improvements outside the recurring operating total.

A fictional comparison illustrates the problem. A lease requires $8,000 in base rent and $2,000 in monthly pass-through charges: $10,000 total. Ownership requires a $7,500 financing payment, $2,200 for taxes and insurance, $900 for routine maintenance, and a $1,500 monthly allocation toward identified future capital work. That is $12,100 before utilities, transaction costs, and any differences in usable space.

The capital allocation is a planning assumption, not a contractor quote or a tax deduction. Replace it with an inspection-based project schedule. A roof replacement in month six cannot be funded merely by writing one-twelfth of its cost into a spreadsheet each month.

Separate liquidity from economic return

The full financing payment affects cash. Principal repayment also reduces the loan balance, so it should be identified separately when comparing long-term wealth effects. Keep appreciation, resale costs, remaining loan balance, and taxes in a separate model with clearly stated assumptions. Do not use hoped-for appreciation to cover a current operating shortfall.

Test a business change

Model what happens if the business needs twice the space, moves its service area, or reduces staffing. Record the time and cash required to exit each alternative. Ownership flexibility depends on market demand, building configuration, legal restrictions, and financing terms; a building is not automatically easy to lease or sell.

The SBA's planning resources emphasize examining leases and financial capacity. Use the bridge with the rent-versus-own guide and take the property assumptions to the lender, inspector, attorney, and tax adviser before deciding.
