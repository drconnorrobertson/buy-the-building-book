Before Buying a Business Building: Map the First 24 Months of Repairs

Create a commercial-building capital project calendar that connects inspection findings, contractor scope, business downtime, and funding dates.

A commercial-property inspection becomes useful to a buyer when its findings change the cash plan, closing conditions, or operating schedule. A list of defects without dates and responsibilities is difficult to use. Turn the findings into a first-24-month project calendar before committing the business to a purchase.

Classify each finding by its effect

Separate conditions that require further investigation from work with a defined scope. A recommendation to investigate water intrusion is not a priced repair. Record what remains unknown, which specialist must assess it, and when the buyer needs the answer.

For each defined project, identify the affected system, urgency, estimated downtime, access needs, permits or approvals to confirm, and responsible party. Let qualified inspectors and local professionals determine safety and regulatory requirements. An owner-created budget should not substitute for their assessment.

Build a scope before comparing prices

Ask contractors to state what their proposals include: demolition, disposal, equipment, labor, controls, testing, restoration, and exclusions. Compare the same scope. A cheaper proposal may exclude electrical work or temporary facilities that the business still needs.

For a fictional HVAC replacement, one estimate might cover equipment and installation while another also includes controls and after-hours work. Place those differences in the calendar and cost schedule. Do not average the bids until the team understands why they differ.

Connect work to business interruption

Schedule dependencies explicitly. Electrical capacity may need verification before equipment is ordered. Roof work may affect planned interior improvements. A project scheduled during the busiest sales month may cost more through lost production than through the contractor invoice.

Estimate disruption in operating terms: appointments displaced, production hours unavailable, inventory requiring protection, or temporary locations needed. Put the interruption assumptions beside the project cost so the business owner can challenge them. Insurance treatment and contractual responsibility require separate review.

Put funding dates ahead of payment dates

List deposits, progress payments, final payment, and any retainage specified in the contract. Confirm when financing or owner funds will actually be available. A repair credit at closing may lower the purchase cash paid without creating a dedicated reserve account for the work.

For uncertain projects, use a range and state what would narrow it. Record a contingency as an explicit assumption. Avoid presenting a round percentage as a guarantee that unexpected conditions are covered.

Use the calendar at the purchase decision

The final review should answer four questions: which findings remain unresolved, which projects must precede occupancy, what cash is required in each quarter, and who will manage the work while the business operates. If these answers change the economics materially, revisit the transaction before closing.
