Sale-Leaseback: Own Your Building and Free Up Capital

The sale-leaseback is one of the most powerful and underutilized structures in commercial real estate. It lets you own your building in a separate entity while your operating company leases it back, creating tax advantages on both sides of the transaction.

The Basic Structure

Here is how it works: You create a separate LLC (your real estate holding company). That LLC purchases the commercial building. Your operating company (the business that occupies the space) signs a lease agreement with the holding LLC at fair market rent.

The result is two separate entities with distinct tax profiles. Your operating company deducts the full rent payment as a business expense. Your holding company collects rental income but offsets it with depreciation, mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance deductions.

Why This Structure Works

The beauty of the sale-leaseback is that you control both sides of the transaction. You are both the landlord and the tenant. This means you can set the lease terms, control improvements, and make decisions without negotiating with an outside party.

From a tax perspective, the holding company can perform a cost segregation study on the building, potentially accelerating $200,000-$500,000 or more in depreciation deductions into the first year of ownership. This creates a paper loss that offsets other income if you qualify as a real estate professional or if you have passive income to shelter.

Key Considerations

The lease must be at fair market value. The IRS will scrutinize related-party leases, so getting a market rent analysis or broker opinion of value is essential. The entities must be properly separated with distinct bank accounts, operating agreements, and arm's-length documentation.

Additionally, consider the financing implications. Lenders will evaluate the holding company's ability to service debt based on the lease income. A long-term lease (10-15 years) with your operating company provides the stability lenders want to see.

The Exit Strategy Advantage

Perhaps the most compelling reason for the sale-leaseback structure: when you eventually sell your operating business, the real estate stays in your holding company. The new business owner signs a lease with you, and you collect rent for years to come without any operational responsibility.

This effectively lets you sell your business at a premium (buyers love not having to worry about real estate) while retaining a passive income stream and an appreciating asset.

A deeper decision framework for Sale

Use this subject as a decision process, not a slogan. Begin by writing the exact action under consideration, the person accountable for it, and the date a conclusion is required. Then separate confirmed facts from estimates and preferences. That simple separation prevents a persuasive narrative from being mistaken for evidence.

Build the working file from leases, loan terms, property records, environmental reports, repair estimates, entity documents, and exit scenarios. Note who prepared each source, the period and entity it covers, and any limitation that could change the conclusion. When two records disagree, preserve the difference and assign a verification step rather than quietly choosing the more favorable number.

Connect the answer to the operating model

The conclusion about sale should change a real term: price, structure, reserves, schedule, staffing, documentation, or the willingness to proceed. If it changes nothing, the team may be researching a topic instead of resolving a decision. Describe the base case, a less favorable case, and the trigger that would move the plan from one to the other.

Review the interaction among occupancy cost, debt coverage, liquidity, control, expansion capacity, alternate use, and exit flexibility. Avoid false precision. A score is useful only when the supporting evidence and judgment are recorded beside it. A weakness may call for a different price, a condition before closing, another specialist, or a capability that must exist before implementation.

Questions that improve the review

  • What evidence would cause the team to change its current conclusion?
  • Which assumption has the largest effect on cash, timing, control, or reversibility?
  • Who owns the next verification step, and what is the deliverable?
  • What happens if cost is 10 percent higher or the benefit arrives one cycle later?
  • What must be preserved so another person can reproduce the reasoning?

After the decision, convert every open item into an owner, deliverable, and date. Store the source material with the implementation record. Schedule a review after execution so the team can compare the assumption with the actual result and improve the next decision.

Review discipline for Sale-Leaseback: Own Your Building and Free Up Capital

Before closing the file, ask someone who did not prepare the original analysis to read the conclusion and identify the assumption with the weakest support. Record that challenge beside the response. Independent review is most valuable when it can still change a term, add a condition, or stop implementation—not after the team is already committed.

Set a review date tied to the first meaningful operating result. Compare the forecast with what actually happened, explain the difference, and update the checklist used for the next decision. The goal is not to prove the original decision was right. It is to make the next review of sale-leaseback: own your building and free up capital faster, clearer, and more accurate.
