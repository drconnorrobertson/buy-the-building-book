How to Calculate Whether Buying Makes Financial Sense

Not every building purchase makes financial sense. Here is the framework for evaluating whether owning your commercial space will generate a positive return, including the factors most people overlook.

The Price-to-Rent Ratio

Start with the basics: divide the building's purchase price by the annual rent you would pay for equivalent space. If the ratio is under 15, buying is likely advantageous. Between 15 and 20, it depends on your tax situation and time horizon. Above 20, renting may be more efficient unless you have strong appreciation expectations.

Example: A $1.2 million building with $96,000 annual rent equivalent = 12.5x ratio. This signals a strong buy opportunity.

Total Cost of Ownership

Your monthly cost as an owner includes more than the mortgage. Factor in: property taxes (typically 1-2% of value annually), insurance ($3,000-$10,000/year), maintenance reserve (1-2% of building value annually), and any property management costs.

Compare this total monthly cost against your rent. If ownership costs are within 10-20% of rent, the equity and tax benefits almost always make up the difference within 2-3 years.

The Tax Multiplier

This is what most analyses miss. When you own the building, you unlock tax deductions (depreciation, interest, cost segregation) that have real cash value. At a 37% marginal tax rate, $100,000 in depreciation deductions saves you $37,000 in actual tax payments.

A cost segregation study in year one might generate $300,000+ in accelerated deductions, saving $100,000+ in taxes. This single benefit can cover your entire down payment.

The 10-Year Wealth Comparison

Project forward 10 years and compare total wealth position under both scenarios. The ownership scenario should include: equity built through principal payments, building appreciation (use 3-5% annually for commercial), cumulative tax savings, and residual value of the asset. The rental scenario should include: total rent paid (with 3% annual escalators) and any investment returns on the down payment you would have invested elsewhere.

In most markets, the ownership scenario wins by $500,000-$2,000,000 over 10 years once tax benefits are properly accounted for.

When Renting Makes More Sense

Building ownership is not always the right choice. Consider renting if: your business may relocate within 3 years, the local market is significantly overpriced (ratio above 25x), your business needs maximum capital flexibility for growth, or the available buildings do not suit your operational needs.

A deeper decision framework for How to Calculate Whether Buying Makes Financial Sense

Use this subject as a decision process, not a slogan. Begin by writing the exact action under consideration, the person accountable for it, and the date a conclusion is required. Then separate confirmed facts from estimates and preferences. That simple separation prevents a persuasive narrative from being mistaken for evidence.

Build the working file from leases, loan terms, property records, environmental reports, repair estimates, entity documents, and exit scenarios. Note who prepared each source, the period and entity it covers, and any limitation that could change the conclusion. When two records disagree, preserve the difference and assign a verification step rather than quietly choosing the more favorable number.

Connect the answer to the operating model

The conclusion about how to calculate whether buying makes financial sense should change a real term: price, structure, reserves, schedule, staffing, documentation, or the willingness to proceed. If it changes nothing, the team may be researching a topic instead of resolving a decision. Describe the base case, a less favorable case, and the trigger that would move the plan from one to the other.

Review the interaction among occupancy cost, debt coverage, liquidity, control, expansion capacity, alternate use, and exit flexibility. Avoid false precision. A score is useful only when the supporting evidence and judgment are recorded beside it. A weakness may call for a different price, a condition before closing, another specialist, or a capability that must exist before implementation.

Questions that improve the review

  • What evidence would cause the team to change its current conclusion?
  • Which assumption has the largest effect on cash, timing, control, or reversibility?
  • Who owns the next verification step, and what is the deliverable?
  • What happens if cost is 10 percent higher or the benefit arrives one cycle later?
  • What must be preserved so another person can reproduce the reasoning?

After the decision, convert every open item into an owner, deliverable, and date. Store the source material with the implementation record. Schedule a review after execution so the team can compare the assumption with the actual result and improve the next decision.

Review discipline for How to Calculate Whether Buying Makes Financial Sense

Before closing the file, ask someone who did not prepare the original analysis to read the conclusion and identify the assumption with the weakest support. Record that challenge beside the response. Independent review is most valuable when it can still change a term, add a condition, or stop implementation—not after the team is already committed.

Set a review date tied to the first meaningful operating result. Compare the forecast with what actually happened, explain the difference, and update the checklist used for the next decision. The goal is not to prove the original decision was right. It is to make the next review of how to calculate whether buying makes financial sense faster, clearer, and more accurate.

Keep specialist advice scoped to a written question and preserve the assumptions the advisor received. Advice can be technically sound and still be misapplied when the underlying facts change. Reconfirm material facts before acting and document any change in the final decision record.
