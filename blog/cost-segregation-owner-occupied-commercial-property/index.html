Cost Segregation for Owner-Occupied Buildings: Review the Study and Tax Assumptions

A cost segregation proposal needs two separate reviews: whether the study supports its asset classifications and costs, and how the resulting tax treatment applies to the actual taxpayer. An owner-occupied building, an operating company and a separate property-owning entity can introduce different facts. A projected deduction is not the same as cash available to the business.

This guide prepares a review conversation with the study provider and tax adviser. It does not calculate a deduction, select a filing method or determine whether a loss can offset a particular category of income.

Start with the building and taxpayer facts

Prepare a short property brief naming the owner, the entity using each part of the building, the acquisition and improvement history, and the relevant tax years. Identify any lease between related entities. Keep the purchase, renovation completion and placed-in-service dates as separate fields, supported by the available records.

IRS Publication 946, How To Depreciate Property, discusses depreciable property, basis, placed-in-service timing and depreciation methods. Ask the tax adviser which edition and current rules apply to the year under review. Commissioning a study does not by itself establish the year a deduction belongs on a return.

Ask how the study supports its conclusions

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Technique Guide, Publication 5653, revised February 2025, describes classification rationale, supporting documentation and reconciliation of allocated costs to actual costs among its study-quality considerations. The guide is an examination resource, not a legal authority or an IRS endorsement of a provider.

  • Scope: which property, improvements and acquisition costs are included, and which are outside the engagement?
  • Evidence: what records, observations and estimates support each material allocation?
  • Classification: where does the report explain the basis for its asset categories and treatment?
  • Reconciliation: how does the allocation tie to the costs being studied, and how are separately acquired assets kept from being counted twice?
  • Limitations: which records are missing, which estimates remain, and who will answer the tax adviser's follow-up questions?

Keep classification separate from tax-use assumptions

Ask the adviser to document the applicable tax year, acquisition and service dates, taxpayer ownership, business use, prior depreciation, elections and any limitations affecting the proposed result. Have the adviser identify the assumptions behind a projection and the circumstances that would change it.

Do not assume that rent paid between related entities makes every depreciation loss usable against operating income. IRS Publication 925, Passive Activity and At-Risk Rules, includes a rule for net rental income from property rented to an activity in which the taxpayer materially participates. That discussion is not a blanket statement that rental losses are nonpassive. The adviser needs the actual ownership, use and participation facts.

If the property was reported on prior returns, ask which prior schedules need review and which filing approach is appropriate. Ask separately about future dispositions and the possible effect of depreciation already claimed. This article supplies no universal bonus-depreciation percentage, catch-up filing instruction or recapture rate.

Fictional example: an improvement may be counted twice

A fictional business bought a building and later paid for an improvement. The proposal's allocation schedule and the owner's fixed-asset list both include an item with a similar description. The names alone do not establish whether they refer to the same asset.

  1. Identify the proposal line, invoice, payment and fixed-asset record involved.
  2. Ask the provider which cost pool includes the item and whether the later work is inside the study scope.
  3. Ask the tax adviser how the item was previously reported and what remains unresolved.
  4. Keep the question open until the records and responsible reviewers support a conclusion. Do not simply remove a line or claim an additional deduction from this example.

The example illustrates a reconciliation question, not a client outcome or an estimate of tax savings.

Bring a defined review package

  • Closing records and available allocation support.
  • Improvement invoices and descriptions of work.
  • Existing fixed-asset and depreciation schedules.
  • Property-use, ownership and lease information.
  • The proposal's scope, fees, assumptions and complete deliverables.
  • A list of unresolved questions, assigned reviewer and requested response date.

Use secure channels for returns and taxpayer information. For the broader property decision, compare the operating assumptions in the occupancy cost bridge and the planned work in the building repair calendar. A tax projection should remain separate from the cash required to acquire and operate the property.
