Owner-occupant real estate playbook

Separate the real estate decision from the romance of ownership

Buying the building can convert a necessary occupancy cost into a durable asset, but only when the property, business, financing, and holding structure work together. This expanded guide helps an owner compare rent and ownership honestly, protect operating flexibility, underwrite the building as its own investment, and plan for a future in which the company and the real estate may follow different paths.

Best for: business owners evaluating whether to keep leasing, buy their current location, or acquire a better facility.

Keep the analysis honest. A building is not automatically a good investment because the business occupies it. Underwrite location, reuse, debt service, capital needs, and exit liquidity independently.

Questions readers ask before they act

When does buying usually deserve a closer look?

When location needs are stable, the business has resilient cash flow, the holding period is long, and the property has viable alternate users.

Should the operating company own the building?

Often owners consider a separate property entity, but the correct structure depends on financing, liability, tax, and succession facts.

How should owner rent be set?

Use a supportable market rate and a written lease; aggressive related-party terms can distort both entities and complicate future transactions.

What is the biggest comparison mistake?

Comparing rent only with principal and interest while ignoring equity, opportunity cost, repairs, taxes, insurance, and transaction costs.

Can seller financing help?

It can bridge valuation or bank constraints, but payment terms, collateral, subordination, defaults, and balloon risk require careful documentation.

What makes a building easier to sell later?

Durable location, flexible configuration, credible maintenance records, clean environmental diligence, and demand from more than one user type.
